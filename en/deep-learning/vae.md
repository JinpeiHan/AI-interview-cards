# VAE

Q1: ***What is the difference between a plain autoencoder (AE) and a variational autoencoder (VAE)?***

- **Autoencoder (AE)**: the encoder maps the input $x$ to a **deterministic** latent vector $z=f(x)$, the decoder reconstructs $\hat x=g(z)$, and it is trained with a reconstruction error.
    - Mainly used for dimensionality reduction, feature learning, and denoising.
    - The latent space is unconstrained and may be discontinuous with "holes"; decoding random samples from the latent space usually does not give meaningful outputs, so it is **not a good generative model**.
- **Variational autoencoder (VAE)**: the encoder outputs a **distribution** $q_\phi(z|x)$ (usually a diagonal Gaussian, outputting mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$), and a KL term pushes it toward the prior $p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I)$.
    - It is a **probabilistic generative model**: after training, new samples can be generated by sampling from $p(z)$ and decoding.
    - The latent space is **continuous and smooth**: nearby latent vectors decode to similar samples, which makes interpolation easy.

Q2: ***What is the generative model assumption of a VAE? Why is variational inference needed?***

- The data are assumed to be generated from latent variables: first sample $z\sim p(z)$, then generate $x$ from $p_\theta(x|z)$. The marginal likelihood is:

$$p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x|z)p(z)\,dz$$

- When $p_\theta(x|z)$ is parameterized by a neural network, this integral is **intractable**, and the posterior $p_\theta(z|x)=\frac{p_\theta(x|z)p(z)}{p_\theta(x)}$ cannot be computed either.
- So an **inference network (encoder)** $q_\phi(z|x)$ is introduced to approximate the true posterior — this is **variational inference**. Since $q_\phi$ is a single network shared across all samples, it is called **amortized inference**.

Q3: ***Derive the VAE ELBO (evidence lower bound).***

- Starting from the log-likelihood and using $q_\phi(z|x)$:

$$\log p_\theta(x)=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\Big]+KL\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z|x)\big)$$

- Since $KL\ge0$, we get the lower bound:

$$\log p_\theta(x)\ge \mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi;x)=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]-KL\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$$

- It can also be derived with **Jensen's inequality**: $\log p_\theta(x)=\log\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\big]\ge\mathbb{E}_q\big[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z|x)}\big]$.
- Meaning of the two terms:
    - **Reconstruction term** $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(x|z)]$: lets the decoder reconstruct $x$ from $z$.
    - **KL regularization term**: pushes the approximate posterior toward the prior, keeping the latent space well-organized so that we can sample from the prior.
- Maximizing the ELBO does two things at once: it increases $\log p_\theta(x)$ and it reduces the KL between $q_\phi(z|x)$ and the true posterior (i.e., **the gap between the bound and the true value**).

Q4: ***What loss does the reconstruction term correspond to in practice?***

- It depends on the assumed decoder distribution $p_\theta(x|z)$:
    - If it is a **Gaussian** (with fixed variance), $-\log p_\theta(x|z)$ is equivalent to a (scaled) **mean squared error (MSE)**.
    - If it is a **Bernoulli** (e.g., binarized MNIST), it corresponds to **binary cross-entropy (BCE)**.
- The expectation is usually estimated with a **single Monte Carlo sample** (one $z$ per data point), which is sufficient with reasonably large batches.
- The variance of a Gaussian decoder determines the relative weight between the reconstruction term and the KL term, playing a role similar to $\beta$ in β-VAE.

Q5: ***What is the reparameterization trick? Why is it needed?***

- The expectation in the ELBO is taken over $q_\phi(z|x)$, and **the sampling operation is not differentiable**, so gradients cannot flow from the decoder through $z$ back to the encoder parameters $\phi$.
- The reparameterization trick moves the randomness to a noise variable that does not depend on the parameters:

$$z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$$

- Now $z$ is a **deterministic, differentiable function** of $\mu,\sigma$; the randomness only comes from $\epsilon$, and gradients can backpropagate through $\mu$ and $\sigma$.
- Compared with REINFORCE (the score-function estimator), reparameterized gradients have **much lower variance**.
- In practice the encoder usually outputs $\log\sigma^2$ instead of $\sigma$, for numerical stability and to keep the variance positive.
- Discrete latent variables cannot be reparameterized directly; **Gumbel-Softmax** or a straight-through estimator can be used instead.

Q6: ***When both the posterior and the prior are Gaussian, what is the closed form of the KL term?***

- Let $q_\phi(z|x)=\mathcal{N}(\mu,\text{diag}(\sigma^2))$, $p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, with latent dimension $J$:

$$KL\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-\log\sigma_j^2-1\big)$$

- This term can be **computed analytically** without sampling, which lowers gradient variance.
- Intuition: the $\mu_j^2$ term pulls the mean toward 0, and $\sigma_j^2-\log\sigma_j^2-1$ reaches its minimum 0 at $\sigma_j=1$, pulling the variance toward 1.

Q7: ***Why are VAE samples often blurry?***

- **Form of the reconstruction loss**: the MSE/L2 loss corresponding to a Gaussian likelihood **averages** over multiple plausible outputs; under uncertainty it outputs the mean, smoothing away high-frequency details.
- **Limitation from KL regularization**: the KL term forces the posteriors of different samples to overlap with the prior, so the encoder cannot encode each sample very precisely, and the decoder can only output an "average" result.
- **Limited expressiveness of the approximate posterior**: a diagonal Gaussian cannot fit the true posterior accurately, leaving a gap between the ELBO and the true likelihood.
- **Mode-covering nature of maximum likelihood**: maximizing likelihood (equivalent to minimizing forward KL) tends to cover all modes, spreading probability mass into low-quality regions between modes.
- Remedies: stronger decoders, hierarchical VAEs (e.g., NVAE), adding perceptual or adversarial losses (VAE-GAN), discrete latents (VQ-VAE), more flexible posteriors (normalizing flows), etc.

Q8: ***What is posterior collapse?***

- Posterior collapse means the approximate posterior degenerates to the prior: $q_\phi(z|x)\approx p(z)$, the KL term goes to 0, **the latent $z$ carries no information about $x$**, and the decoder ignores $z$.
- It is common when the **decoder is very powerful**, e.g., autoregressive decoders (LSTM, PixelCNN, Transformer) that can model $x$ well on their own without using $z$.
- Early in training, the KL term is easier to optimize than the reconstruction term, so the model may fall into the "KL = 0" local optimum first.

Q9: ***How can posterior collapse be mitigated?***

- **KL annealing / warm-up**: gradually increase the KL weight from 0 to 1 early in training, so the model first learns to use $z$.
- **Free bits**: set a floor $\lambda$ on the KL of each latent dimension and stop penalizing below it, i.e., $\max(\lambda, KL_j)$, encouraging every dimension to encode at least some information.
- **Weaken the decoder**: use a weaker decoder, or apply word dropout to the decoder input (common in text VAEs), forcing it to rely on $z$.
- **Change the objective/architecture**: δ-VAE (enforcing a minimum distance between posterior and prior), hierarchical latents, skip connections that let $z$ affect the output more directly, etc.
- Discrete latents (e.g., VQ-VAE) also avoid this problem structurally.

Q10: ***What is β-VAE? How does it relate to disentangled representations?***

- β-VAE puts a coefficient $\beta$ in front of the KL term of the ELBO:

$$\mathcal{L}_\beta=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]-\beta\,KL\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$$

- **$\beta>1$**: imposes a stronger information bottleneck on the latents and pushes the posterior more strongly toward an isotropic Gaussian prior **with independent dimensions**, encouraging each latent dimension to correspond to a single independent factor of variation (e.g., position, scale, rotation), i.e., **disentanglement**.
- **Cost**: the larger $\beta$, the worse the reconstruction and the blurrier the samples; one must trade off disentanglement against reconstruction quality.
- **$\beta<1$**: better reconstruction, but a less regular latent space.
- Follow-up work such as FactorVAE and β-TCVAE further decomposes the KL and specifically penalizes the **total correlation** among latent variables.
- Note: unsupervised disentanglement lacks identifiability guarantees, and in practice its success depends on inductive biases and hyperparameters.

Q11: ***What is the core idea of VQ-VAE?***

- VQ-VAE (Vector Quantized VAE) uses **discrete latent variables**:
    - The encoder outputs a continuous vector $z_e(x)$.
    - A **codebook** $\{e_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$ is maintained, and $z_e(x)$ is replaced by the nearest code vector: $z_q(x)=e_k,\ k=\arg\min_j\|z_e(x)-e_j\|_2$.
    - The decoder reconstructs $x$ from $z_q(x)$.
- During training the prior is treated as **uniform**, so the KL term is a constant and ignored; after training, an autoregressive model (e.g., PixelCNN, Transformer) is **fit as the prior** over the discrete codes for generation.
- Advantages: avoids posterior collapse; discrete representations pair well with autoregressive/Transformer models; sharp reconstructions.
- Applications: VQ-VAE-2, the discrete image tokenizer in the first-generation DALL·E, VQGAN, audio codecs, etc.

Q12: ***Quantization in VQ-VAE is non-differentiable — how is it trained? What is the loss function?***

- **Straight-through estimator**: the forward pass uses the quantized $z_q$, and in the backward pass the decoder's gradient with respect to $z_q$ is **copied directly** to $z_e$, skipping the non-differentiable argmin. It is often implemented as $z_q=z_e+\text{sg}(z_q-z_e)$.
- Total loss:

$$L=-\log p(x|z_q(x))+\|\text{sg}[z_e(x)]-e\|_2^2+\beta\|z_e(x)-\text{sg}[e]\|_2^2$$

- Here $\text{sg}$ denotes **stop-gradient**; the three terms are:
    - **Reconstruction loss**: trains the encoder and decoder.
    - **Codebook loss**: pulls the code vectors $e$ toward the encoder outputs; it only updates the codebook.
    - **Commitment loss**: pulls the encoder outputs toward the chosen code vectors, preventing the encoder outputs from growing without bound or jumping frequently between codes; it only updates the encoder, and $\beta$ is usually small (0.25 in the original paper).
- The codebook can also be updated with an **exponential moving average (EMA)** instead of the codebook loss.
- A common problem is **low codebook utilization (codebook collapse)**, which can be mitigated by resetting dead codes, EMA updates, low-dimensional/normalized codebooks, etc.

Q13: ***What role does the VAE play in latent diffusion models (Latent Diffusion / Stable Diffusion)?***

- A latent diffusion model first trains an **autoencoder (VAE)** to compress images into a low-dimensional latent space (e.g., downsampling spatial resolution by 8×), then trains a diffusion model in this **latent space**, and finally uses the decoder to map generated latents back to images.
- Its roles:
    - **Greatly reduces computation**: the diffusion model operates on smaller tensors, so both training and sampling are faster.
    - **Clear division of labor**: the VAE handles perceptual compression (removing high-frequency details the eye is insensitive to), while the diffusion model handles semantic-level generative modeling.
- The VAE here usually uses only a **very small KL weight** (or VQ regularization), so that the latent variance stays moderate and easy to model with diffusion while reconstruction quality stays high; training also commonly adds **perceptual and adversarial (patch discriminator) losses** to avoid blurry reconstructions.
- The VAE's reconstruction quality sets an upper bound on the fine details (e.g., text, facial details) the whole system can generate.

Q14: ***What is a conditional VAE (CVAE)?***

- A condition $c$ (e.g., class label, partial observation) is introduced into the encoder, the decoder, and the prior:

$$\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x,c)}[\log p_\theta(x|z,c)]-KL\big(q_\phi(z|x,c)\,\|\,p_\psi(z|c)\big)$$

- The prior can be the fixed $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ or a learnable condition-dependent prior $p_\psi(z|c)$.
- At generation time, given condition $c$, sample $z$ from the prior and decode to obtain a sample satisfying the condition, enabling **controllable generation**; different $z$ under the same condition produce **diverse outputs** (useful for one-to-many mappings such as trajectory prediction and image inpainting).

Q15: ***What are the properties of the VAE latent space? How can it be used for interpolation and attribute editing?***

- Because the KL term pulls every sample's posterior toward the same prior, the latent space is **continuous and compact**, and points in high-probability regions of the prior decode to plausible samples.
- **Interpolation**: linearly (or spherically) interpolate between the latent vectors of two samples; decoding gives a smooth transition.
- **Attribute editing**: compute the difference between the mean latent vectors of samples with and without an attribute to get an "attribute vector"; adding it to another sample's latent vector edits that attribute.
- The more disentangled the latent space (e.g., β-VAE), the clearer the semantics of individual dimensions and the more controllable the editing.

Q16: ***What are the pros and cons of VAEs compared with GANs?***

- **VAE pros**:
    - Stable training with a clear optimization objective (the ELBO), free of adversarial-training instability.
    - Has an encoder, enabling **inference** (from $x$ to $z$), and a well-structured latent space.
    - Provides a lower bound on the log-likelihood, which can be used to compare models.
    - Better mode coverage; less prone to mode collapse.
- **VAE cons**:
    - Blurrier samples; image quality is usually below GANs.
    - Posterior collapse may occur.
- **Combinations**: VAE-GAN uses a discriminator to replace or supplement pixel-level reconstruction loss; VQGAN adds adversarial and perceptual losses on top of VQ-VAE for sharper results.

Q17: ***What is IWAE? How can the ELBO be made tighter?***

- **IWAE (Importance Weighted Autoencoder)** uses importance weighting over $K$ samples to build a tighter lower bound:

$$\mathcal{L}_K=\mathbb{E}_{z_1,\dots,z_K\sim q_\phi(z|x)}\Big[\log\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{p_\theta(x,z_k)}{q_\phi(z_k|x)}\Big]$$

- With $K=1$ it is the standard ELBO; larger $K$ gives a tighter bound, converging to $\log p_\theta(x)$ as $K\to\infty$.
- Other ways to tighten the bound: more flexible approximate posteriors such as **normalizing flows** (e.g., IAF), and hierarchical latents (Ladder VAE, NVAE).
- IWAE-style estimators are commonly used to **evaluate** the log-likelihood of VAEs.
