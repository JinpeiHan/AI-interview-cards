# VAE

Q1: ***What is the difference between a plain autoencoder (AE) and a variational autoencoder (VAE)?***

- **An AE encodes the input into a single deterministic vector and only aims to reconstruct it; its latent space is unconstrained, so it cannot be sampled for generation. A VAE encodes the input into a distribution and uses a KL term to pull it toward a standard Gaussian prior, so the latent space is continuous and smooth and new samples can be generated by sampling from the prior.**
- AEs are mainly used for dimensionality reduction, feature learning and denoising; their latent space can be discontinuous with "holes", so decoding random samples usually gives nothing meaningful.
- A VAE is a probabilistic generative model: the encoder outputs a mean and variance, and after training you sample from $p(z)$ and decode to generate.
- In a VAE latent space, nearby vectors decode to similar samples, which makes interpolation easy.

Q2: ***What is the generative model assumption of a VAE? Why is variational inference needed?***

- **A VAE assumes data is generated from a latent variable: first sample $z$ from the prior, then the decoder generates $x$. Since both the integral over $z$ and the true posterior are intractable, an encoder is used to approximate the posterior; this is variational inference.**
- When the decoder is a neural network, the marginal likelihood integral has no closed form, and the posterior $p_\theta(z|x)$ cannot be computed either.
- The encoder $q_\phi(z|x)$ is a single network shared across all samples, which is called amortized inference.
- Key formula: $p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x|z)p(z)\,dz$

Q3: ***Derive the VAE ELBO (evidence lower bound).***

- **The log-likelihood equals the ELBO plus the KL between the approximate posterior and the true posterior; since KL is non-negative, the ELBO is a lower bound on the log-likelihood (it can also be obtained directly from Jensen's inequality).**
- Reconstruction term: makes the decoder able to reconstruct $x$ from $z$.
- KL regularization term: keeps the approximate posterior close to the prior, so the latent space is well organized and can be sampled from the prior.
- Maximizing the ELBO does two things at once: it raises the likelihood and shrinks the gap between the approximate and true posteriors (the gap between the bound and the true value).
- Key formula: $\log p_\theta(x)\ge\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]-KL\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$

Q4: ***What loss does the reconstruction term correspond to in practice?***

- **It depends on the assumed decoder distribution: a Gaussian (fixed variance) gives MSE, and a Bernoulli (e.g. binarized MNIST) gives binary cross-entropy (BCE).**
- The expectation is usually estimated with a single Monte Carlo sample (one $z$ per data point), which is enough with a reasonably large batch.
- The variance of a Gaussian decoder sets the relative weight of the reconstruction and KL terms, acting like $\beta$ in β-VAE.

Q5: ***What is the reparameterization trick? Why is it needed?***

- **Sampling itself is not differentiable, so gradients cannot flow back to the encoder; reparameterization moves the randomness into a parameter-free standard Gaussian noise, making $z$ a differentiable function of the mean and variance so gradients can backpropagate.**
- Compared with REINFORCE (the score-function estimator), reparameterized gradients have much lower variance.
- In practice the encoder usually outputs $\log\sigma^2$ for numerical stability and to keep the variance positive.
- Discrete latents cannot be reparameterized directly; use Gumbel-Softmax or a straight-through estimator.
- Key formula: $z=\mu+\sigma\odot\epsilon,\ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$

Q6: ***When both the posterior and the prior are Gaussian, what is the closed form of the KL term?***

- **The KL between a diagonal Gaussian posterior and a standard Gaussian prior has a closed form, so it can be computed directly instead of estimated by sampling, giving lower gradient variance.**
- Intuition: $\mu_j^2$ pulls the mean toward 0; $\sigma_j^2-\log\sigma_j^2-1$ is minimized at $\sigma_j=1$, pulling the variance toward 1.
- Key formula: $KL=\frac{1}{2}\sum_j\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-\log\sigma_j^2-1\big)$

Q7: ***Why are VAE samples often blurry?***

- **Mainly because an MSE-style reconstruction loss outputs the average of several possible results when uncertain, and the KL regularization keeps encodings imprecise, so the decoder can only output an "average" image and high-frequency details are smoothed away.**
- The KL term forces different samples' posteriors to overlap with the prior, so the encoder cannot encode each sample precisely.
- A diagonal Gaussian posterior has limited expressiveness, leaving a gap between the ELBO and the true likelihood.
- The mode-covering nature of maximum likelihood spreads probability mass into low-quality regions between modes.
- Remedies: stronger decoders, hierarchical VAEs (NVAE), perceptual/adversarial losses (VAE-GAN), discrete latents (VQ-VAE), more flexible posteriors (normalizing flows).

Q8: ***What is posterior collapse?***

- **Posterior collapse means the approximate posterior degenerates into the prior: the KL term goes to 0, the latent $z$ carries no information about the input, and the decoder ignores $z$ entirely.**
- It is common when the decoder is very powerful, such as autoregressive decoders (LSTM, PixelCNN, Transformer), which can model $x$ on their own without $z$.
- Early in training the KL term is easier to optimize than the reconstruction term, so the model may first fall into a "KL = 0" local optimum.

Q9: ***How can posterior collapse be mitigated?***

- **The core idea is to make the model learn to use $z$ first: e.g. KL annealing, a lower bound on the KL (free bits), weakening the decoder, or switching to discrete latents.**
- KL annealing (warm-up): the KL weight is increased gradually from 0 to 1 early in training.
- Free bits: no penalty when a latent dimension's KL is below a floor $\lambda$, encouraging each dimension to encode at least some information.
- Weaken the decoder: use a weaker decoder, or apply word dropout to the decoder input (common in text VAEs), forcing it to rely on $z$.
- Change the objective/architecture: δ-VAE, hierarchical latents, skip connections; the discrete latents of VQ-VAE also avoid collapse structurally.

Q10: ***What is β-VAE? How does it relate to disentangled representations?***

- **β-VAE simply multiplies the KL term by a coefficient $\beta$; with $\beta>1$ the information bottleneck is stronger and the posterior is pushed harder toward the factorized prior, encouraging each latent dimension to capture one independent factor of variation, i.e. a disentangled representation, at the cost of worse reconstruction.**
- For example, different dimensions control position, size and rotation separately.
- With $\beta<1$ reconstruction is better but the latent space is less regular; there is a trade-off between disentanglement and reconstruction quality.
- FactorVAE and β-TCVAE further decompose the KL and specifically penalize the total correlation among latents.
- Unsupervised disentanglement lacks identifiability guarantees; results depend on inductive biases and hyperparameters.
- Key formula: $\mathcal{L}_\beta=\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(x|z)]-\beta\,KL\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$

Q11: ***What is the core idea of VQ-VAE?***

- **VQ-VAE uses discrete latents: the encoder's continuous output vector is replaced by the nearest codeword in a codebook and then reconstructed by the decoder; after training, an autoregressive model learns a prior over the discrete codes for generation.**
- During training the prior is treated as uniform, so the KL term is a constant and dropped; for generation, PixelCNN, a Transformer or similar fits the code distribution.
- Advantages: avoids posterior collapse; discrete representations pair well with autoregressive/Transformer models; sharp reconstructions.
- Applications: VQ-VAE-2, the image tokenizer of the first DALL·E, VQGAN, audio codecs.

Q12: ***Quantization in VQ-VAE is non-differentiable — how is it trained? What is the loss function?***

- **With a straight-through estimator: the forward pass uses the quantized vector, and the backward pass copies the gradient directly to the encoder output, skipping the non-differentiable argmin; the loss has three parts: reconstruction loss, codebook loss and commitment loss.**
- The reconstruction loss trains the encoder and decoder; the codebook loss pulls codewords toward encoder outputs (updates only the codebook).
- The commitment loss pulls encoder outputs toward their chosen codewords, preventing unbounded growth or frequent jumping between codewords (updates only the encoder; $\beta$ is 0.25 in the original paper).
- The codebook can also be updated by EMA instead of the codebook loss; low codebook utilization (codebook collapse) can be mitigated by codeword resets, EMA, or low-dimensional/normalized codebooks.
- Key formula: $L=-\log p(x|z_q)+\|\text{sg}[z_e]-e\|^2+\beta\|z_e-\text{sg}[e]\|^2$

Q13: ***What role does the VAE play in latent diffusion models (Latent Diffusion / Stable Diffusion)?***

- **The VAE compresses images into a low-dimensional latent space (e.g. 8x spatial downsampling), the diffusion model generates in that latent space, and the VAE decoder maps the result back to an image; this greatly reduces compute.**
- Clear division of labor: the VAE does perceptual compression (removing high-frequency details the eye is insensitive to), and the diffusion model does semantic-level generation.
- This VAE uses only a very small KL weight (or VQ regularization), keeping latent variance moderate and reconstruction quality high; training also adds perceptual and patch-discriminator adversarial losses to avoid blur.
- The VAE's reconstruction quality caps how fine the details of the whole system can be (text, faces).

Q14: ***What is a conditional VAE (CVAE)?***

- **A CVAE adds a condition $c$ (e.g. a class label or partial observation) to the encoder, decoder and prior; at generation time, given the condition, sampling $z$ yields outputs that satisfy the condition and are diverse.**
- The prior can be a fixed standard Gaussian or a learnable condition-dependent prior $p_\psi(z|c)$.
- Different $z$ under the same condition give different outputs, which suits one-to-many problems such as trajectory prediction and image completion.
- Key formula: $\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x,c)}[\log p_\theta(x|z,c)]-KL\big(q_\phi(z|x,c)\,\|\,p_\psi(z|c)\big)$

Q15: ***What are the properties of the VAE latent space? How can it be used for interpolation and attribute editing?***

- **Because the KL term pulls every sample's posterior toward the same prior, the VAE latent space is continuous and compact, so interpolating in it or adding/subtracting "attribute vectors" still decodes to plausible samples.**
- Interpolation: linearly or spherically interpolate between two samples' latents and decode to get a smooth transition.
- Attribute editing: take the difference between mean latents of samples with and without an attribute as an attribute vector, and add it to other samples to edit that attribute.
- The more disentangled the space (e.g. β-VAE), the clearer each dimension's meaning and the more controllable the edits.

Q16: ***What are the pros and cons of VAEs compared with GANs?***

- **VAEs train stably, have an encoder for inference and cover modes well, but samples are often blurry; GANs give sharper samples but train unstably and are prone to mode collapse.**
- A VAE has an explicit objective (the ELBO) and provides a log-likelihood lower bound for model comparison.
- Another VAE drawback is possible posterior collapse.
- Combining them: VAE-GAN uses a discriminator to replace or supplement the pixel reconstruction loss; VQGAN adds adversarial and perceptual losses to VQ-VAE for sharper generation.

Q17: ***What is IWAE? How can the ELBO be made tighter?***

- **IWAE draws $K$ samples from the approximate posterior, averages their importance weights and then takes the log, giving a tighter bound than the ELBO; $K=1$ is the standard ELBO, and larger $K$ gets closer to the true log-likelihood.**
- Other ways to tighten the bound: more flexible approximate posteriors (normalizing flows such as IAF) and hierarchical latents (Ladder VAE, NVAE).
- IWAE-style estimators are commonly used to evaluate a VAE's log-likelihood.
- Key formula: $\mathcal{L}_K=\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{p_\theta(x,z_k)}{q_\phi(z_k|x)}\Big]$
